INTERVENTION: agroforestry
METRIC: biodiversity
SOURCE: FAO Agroforestry Overview
SOURCE_URL: https://www.fao.org/agroforestry/about-agroforestry/overview/
REGION: general
---
Agroforestry deliberately integrates woody perennials with crops and/or animals
in a spatial arrangement or temporal sequence. FAO frames agroforestry as a land
management approach that can diversify production while strengthening ecological
and socio-economic interactions among system components. For biodiversity, the
main advantage is structural diversity: trees, shrubs, crops, forage, leaf
litter, roots, and shaded microhabitats support more niches than a simplified
annual monoculture.

Soil organic carbon can also improve where trees increase belowground biomass,
add leaf litter, reduce erosion, and maintain perennial cover. These gains are
not automatic. They depend on species choice, planting density, pruning,
competition for water and light, and whether residues are retained or removed.
Agroforestry is especially relevant in degraded landscapes where long-lived
woody vegetation can rebuild habitat continuity and provide shade, fodder,
fruit, fuelwood, or timber. Good design avoids simply replacing one monoculture
with another. Mixed, locally adapted systems usually offer stronger resilience
than single-species tree rows managed only for one product. FAO summarizes that
some agroforestry systems can support roughly 50-80 percent of the biodiversity
found in natural forests, a useful benchmark but not a guarantee for simplified
or poorly managed plantings.
