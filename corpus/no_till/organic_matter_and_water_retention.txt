No-till can contribute to water retention when it is paired with surface residue
and diverse crop cover. FAO describes conservation agriculture as reducing
excessive soil disturbance while maintaining residues and cover crops on the
surface. This combination helps rainfall enter the soil, reduces runoff, and
supports biological activity that rebuilds soil architecture. The water benefit
comes from both physical protection at the surface and gradual improvement of
soil organic matter and aggregation.

The response varies by soil texture, residue level, climate, and management
history. Fine-textured soils may need careful traffic management to avoid
compaction, while sandy soils may show quicker changes in infiltration but lower
capacity to store water. In semi-arid areas, retaining residues can reduce
evaporation and protect seedlings from heat, but residue production may be
limited by low biomass. No-till systems should therefore be assessed with
practical indicators: infiltration after rainfall, visible crusting, residue
cover, root depth, and crop stress during dry periods. Long-term success usually
requires the full conservation agriculture package: minimal disturbance, soil
cover, and diversified rotations. USDA ARS research in Georgia reported that
conservation tillage limited rainfall runoff to less than 10 percent in test
plots and increased infiltration by up to 50 percent compared with conventional
tillage in some cases.
