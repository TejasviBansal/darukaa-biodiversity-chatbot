No-till and reduced-till systems protect soil structure by minimizing mechanical
disturbance. USDA NRCS soil health guidance describes minimizing disturbance as
one of the principles for cropland soil health, noting that tillage can damage
soil organic matter, structure, biological habitat, infiltration, and erosion
resistance. When soil is left less disturbed, aggregates and pore networks can
develop more continuously, especially when combined with living roots and soil
cover.

No-till is most effective as part of a system rather than as an isolated
practice. Residue retention protects the surface, cover crops add roots and
organic matter, and rotations reduce pest and weed risks. In fields with a long
history of compaction, drainage problems, or heavy residue removal, benefits may
take time and may require transitional management. Weed control can also become
more challenging if the system relies only on herbicides. The ecological goal is
not simply to stop plowing, but to reduce disturbance while increasing cover,
biological activity, and residue inputs. Under those conditions, no-till can
reduce runoff, improve infiltration, and lower erosion vulnerability. FAO
reports residue-based zero tillage trials in Brazil and Paraguay, with
commonly reported soil-loss reductions in the 80-95 percent range across
several zero-tillage systems in the region, though maize results were more
modest in one central Brazil comparison.
