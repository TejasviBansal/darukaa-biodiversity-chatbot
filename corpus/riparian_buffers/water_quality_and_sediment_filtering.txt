Riparian forest buffers are corridors of trees and shrubs planted next to
streams, rivers, wetlands, or other water bodies. USDA NRCS describes their
primary purposes as protecting near-stream soils, trapping chemicals or sediment
from surface and subsurface flows, and providing shade, detritus, and woody
debris for aquatic ecosystems. These functions make riparian buffers one of the
most direct biodiversity interventions for farms connected to waterways.

The water-quality benefit comes from slowing runoff, increasing infiltration,
stabilizing banks, and filtering sediment and nutrients before they enter the
stream. Tree roots and perennial vegetation also help hold streambanks in place.
Shade can moderate water temperature, which matters for aquatic species sensitive
to heat stress. Buffer width, slope, soil type, vegetation density, and upstream
management all affect performance. A narrow strip may provide some bank
stability, while wider and more diverse buffers generally support stronger
habitat and filtering functions. Livestock exclusion, controlled crossings, and
alternative watering points often make buffers more effective by preventing
trampling and direct manure deposition in the stream corridor. NRCS describes
typical riparian forest buffer widths of about 15-100 feet, and USDA-linked
extension guidance commonly cites sediment and nutrient reductions in the
70-95%+ range depending on buffer width, vegetation, slope, and flow path.
