Windbreaks and buffer strips can function as more than erosion-control
structures. The USDA National Agroforestry Center describes windbreaks as linear
plantings of trees and shrubs that benefit soils, crops, livestock, wildlife,
and people. Beyond slowing wind, they can provide wildlife habitat, pollinator
resources, carbon storage, air-quality protection, and soil and water quality
benefits.

For biodiversity, the value of a windbreak depends on plant diversity,
flowering sequence, structural layers, and connection to other habitat patches.
A single-species row may slow wind but provide fewer ecological functions than
a mixed planting with trees, shrubs, grasses, and flowering plants. Buffers can
also intercept dust and pesticide drift, which is relevant near sensitive
habitats or organic production fields. Management should avoid turning the
windbreak into an unmanaged weed source; periodic inspection, replanting gaps,
and controlling invasive plants are part of maintaining function. In simplified
farmland, even narrow woody strips can improve movement corridors for birds,
beneficial insects, and small wildlife, especially when linked to riparian
areas, hedgerows, or remnant natural vegetation. FAO water-management guidance
reports that reducing wind velocity by 50 percent, for example from 32 to
16 km/h, can reduce crop evapotranspiration by about 33 percent under windy dry
conditions.
