Field windbreaks are rows of trees or shrubs planted across prevailing damaging
winds to reduce wind speed in the protected area. USDA NRCS describes their
primary purposes as controlling wind erosion, distributing snow, improving crop
quality and production, and improving water use efficiency. By slowing wind at
the soil surface, windbreaks reduce the movement of fine particles that often
contain organic matter and nutrients.

Windbreak performance depends on height, density, continuity, orientation, and
species selection. NRCS design guidance commonly describes moderately porous
barriers rather than solid walls because some air movement through the planting
creates a longer, more useful wind shadow. In exposed drylands, the soil
protection value can be substantial, especially where crop residues are low or
fields are vulnerable after tillage. Windbreaks also require management: gaps,
dead trees, or poorly adapted species reduce effectiveness. Root competition and
shading near the tree row should be considered when choosing crops and spacing.
When designed well, windbreaks add perennial habitat while protecting cropland
from wind-driven erosion and moisture loss. NRCS field windbreak guidance gives
a design target of 35-60 percent barrier density during the erosion period, with
windbreak intervals commonly spaced about 5H to 20H apart, where H is tree
height.
