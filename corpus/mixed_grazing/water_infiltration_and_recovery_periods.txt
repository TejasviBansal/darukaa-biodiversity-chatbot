Grazing systems influence water infiltration through their effects on soil
cover, root growth, compaction, and plant recovery. USDA NRCS notes that
overgrazing reduces infiltration capacity, increases runoff and flooding, and
can lead to declining plant production and shifts in species composition. Mixed
or rotational grazing can improve outcomes when it allows plants enough time to
recover and rebuild leaf area and roots before being grazed again.

Recovery periods are central because root growth and soil biological activity
depend on photosynthesis. If animals return too quickly, plants lose vigor,
bare patches expand, and hoof impact can compact wet soils. If grazing is timed
well, manure and plant residues feed soil organisms, roots maintain pore spaces,
and cover moderates soil temperature. Mixed grazing may distribute use across
grasses, forbs, shrubs, or browse, but it still requires careful stocking and
movement. Water points and shade should be placed to avoid repeatedly damaging
the same streambanks or low areas. Useful field indicators include infiltration
after storms, hoof pugging, litter cover, plant recovery height, and whether
desirable forage species are increasing or declining.
