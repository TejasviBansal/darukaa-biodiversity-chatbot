SOURCE: FAO Conservation Agriculture Species Diversification

---
Crop rotation supports nutrient cycling by changing the rooting patterns,
residue quality, and biological inputs that enter the soil over time. FAO
explains that rotations offer a diverse diet to soil microorganisms and allow
roots at different depths to explore different soil layers. Some rotation crops
can recover nutrients that have moved deeper in the profile, while legumes can
contribute nitrogen through symbiosis with soil biota.

A rotation is more than a sequence of market crops. It is a way to manage soil
biology, residues, weeds, pests, and water use across seasons. Deep-rooted crops
can create biopores, fibrous-rooted crops can stabilize aggregates near the
surface, and high-residue crops can add organic matter. The strongest soil
health benefits usually appear when rotations include functionally different
species rather than minor variations of the same crop family. Rotations should
also account for local disease risks, residue decomposition rates, livestock
feed needs, and planting windows. In simplified monocultures, adding even one
well-chosen break crop can improve biological diversity, but broader rotations
offer more reliable long-term resilience. FAO conservation agriculture guidance
sets a practical diversification benchmark of at least three different crops in
rotation or association, which helps prevent treating a two-crop sequence as a
fully diversified system.
