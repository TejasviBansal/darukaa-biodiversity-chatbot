Crop rotation is a core strategy for reducing the carry-over of crop-specific
pests, diseases, and weeds. FAO conservation agriculture guidance notes that
rotation has an important phytosanitary function because it interrupts the
movement of pests and diseases from one crop to the next. This is especially
important where the same host crop is planted repeatedly and pests can complete
their life cycles without disruption.

The most useful rotations separate crops from the same family, vary planting
and harvest timing, and include species with different residue and rooting
characteristics. A break crop may reduce disease inoculum, shift weed pressure,
or create a window for non-chemical weed control. Rotations can also support
beneficial organisms by providing diverse root exudates and residues. Results
depend on the biology of the target pest and the length of the break. Some
soil-borne pathogens persist for years, while other pests respond quickly to a
single non-host crop. Good rotation planning therefore starts with the local
problem: pest, weed, nutrient imbalance, erosion, livestock feed gap, or soil
carbon depletion. The rotation should then be designed around that constraint.
