METRIC: ground_cover

Intercropping can improve ground cover by filling spaces between crop rows and
reducing the time that soil remains bare. FAO describes conservation agriculture
as combining minimum soil disturbance, permanent organic soil cover, and crop
species diversification in sequences or associations. Intercropping contributes
most directly to the diversification and cover principles when plant mixtures
are chosen to complement each other rather than compete strongly for the same
resources at the same time.

The ecological logic is resource partitioning. A cereal and legume, or a tall
crop and low-growing cover crop, may use light, rooting depth, and nutrients in
different ways. More continuous cover can protect soil from raindrop impact,
moderate temperature, and provide habitat for soil organisms. The benefit is
strongest where the secondary crop establishes early enough to cover the ground
but is managed so it does not suppress the main crop. In low-rainfall systems,
plant density and termination timing matter because extra cover can also use
scarce water. Intercropping should therefore be evaluated with both biodiversity
and production indicators: cover percentage, residue return, pest incidence,
water stress, and yield stability.
